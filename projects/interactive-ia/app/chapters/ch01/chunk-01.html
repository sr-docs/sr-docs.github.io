Defining Information Architecture & Its Goals

Richard Saul Wurman coined the term "information architecture" in 1975, but it was Lou Rosenfeld and Peter Morville who, in 1998, defined it as a practice — one focused on organizing content, assigning clear labels, and designing navigation and search systems so people can find what they need. That practical, structure-first definition is the one this course uses throughout.

IA exists to achieve specific goals: making information easy to find, providing information scent so people know they're on the right path, supporting both browsing and search, creating a sense of place within a digital space, and combating information overload. Every structural decision an information architect makes should trace back to one or more of these goals.

IA is not the visual design of a page. It's the underlying decisions about what content exists, how it's grouped, and how people can find it — decisions that hold up regardless of what the page eventually looks like.

Exercise

The scenario: A product team is building a new internal employee handbook portal. In a planning meeting, four different statements come up. Classify each one by the discipline it most directly reflects.

1. "We're deciding whether policies should be grouped by department or by topic, and what to call each group."
2. "We're choosing the typeface and color palette for the new portal so it matches the brand guidelines."
3. "We're figuring out what tone the HR policies should be written in, and who's responsible for keeping them updated."
4. "We're deciding how many levels deep the navigation should go before someone gets frustrated looking for their expense policy."
Chunk complete
Feedback

Statement 1 — Information Architecture. Deciding how to group content and what to call the groups is a direct organizational-structure and labeling decision — the core of IA work.

Statement 2 — Visual Design. Typeface and color are presentation decisions. They affect how the portal looks, not how its content is structured or found.

Statement 3 — Content Strategy. Tone of voice and ownership of ongoing updates are content strategy concerns — they're about the content itself and its lifecycle, not its structure.

Statement 4 — Information Architecture. This is a breadth-versus-depth decision in the navigation hierarchy — a defining IA concern covered in depth later in this course.

The overlap in statement 3 is real. Content strategy defines what the content says and who maintains it; IA defines how that content is classified and labeled so it can be found. In practice, the same person often does both in smaller organizations — but they remain distinct decisions. A content strategist could pick a perfect tone of voice for a policy that's still buried under the wrong category label. Getting the label right is IA's job; getting the tone right is content strategy's job.

This distinction matters because it tells you what to fix when something goes wrong. If users can't find a policy, that's an IA problem — check labels, categories, and navigation. If users find the policy but don't trust or understand it, that's more likely a content strategy problem — check tone, clarity, and currency.

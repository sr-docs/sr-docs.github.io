Building on What You Find: Pearl-Growing and Sensemaking

Citation pearl growing, described by Sheryl Ramer, uses a strong initial source — the "pearl" — as a starting point: the searcher notes its keywords or index terms, then uses those terms to find more similar sources, repeating the process to grow more pearls. It's a way of using what you've already found to find more of what you need.

Finding information is only half the task — people also need to make sense of it. Brenda Dervin's sensemaking model describes this as bridging a gap: a person is in a situation, encounters a gap in their understanding, and needs to construct a bridge to it using information, which they then put to some use. Peter Pirolli and Stuart Card extended this into a two-loop model: an information-foraging loop, where the person gathers and skims raw material, and a sensemaking loop, where they build a schema — a mental or written representation — that helps them draw conclusions from what they've gathered.

A good source doesn't just answer a question — it hands the searcher new keywords, new connections, and new starting points, which is exactly what makes pearl-growing possible.

Exercise

The scenario: A graduate student, Wren, is deep into a literature review on urban heat island effects. She's found one excellent, highly-cited paper that precisely matches her topic. She's now trying to build outward from it — finding related work — while also trying to organize the growing pile of PDFs and notes into something coherent enough to write from.

Which single feature would most directly support Wren's ability to grow more pearls from the paper she's already found?

Feedback

The related articles / cited by list is correct. This is pearl-growing made concrete: the paper Wren already trusts becomes the pearl, and a feature that surfaces its keywords, citations, and citing works lets her grow outward from it without having to reformulate a fresh search query from scratch. The other three options — color scheme, font size, live chat — are all reasonable usability improvements in general, but none of them help her find more sources from the one she's already found.

Finding more sources and making sense of what you've found are two different design problems, and it's easy to conflate them. A related-articles feature solves the first. It does nothing for the second — Wren can have twenty excellent PDFs and still be no closer to understanding how they relate to each other or what her literature review should actually argue.

Features that support the sensemaking side include annotation or highlighting tools that persist and are searchable across all her saved sources, a way to tag and group sources by theme rather than just by date saved, a personal workspace or collection feature that lets her arrange sources spatially or thematically, and export to a citation manager that preserves her own notes alongside each reference. All of these help her build the schema Pirolli and Card describe — a structure she constructs herself, out of raw material the foraging loop already gathered.

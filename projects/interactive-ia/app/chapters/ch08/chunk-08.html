Documenting and Presenting IA Strategy

An IA strategy document typically opens with a concise overview for busy executives who won't read the full document — key issues, recommendations, and business impact, stated up front rather than buried at the end. From there, detailed recommendations are organized by issue, each connecting a specific finding to its analysis and its strategic implications, before moving into the fuller detail of organizational structure, labeling, navigation, and content requirements.

Even when a team has already been closely involved in workshops and discussions, a written document alone rarely ensures busy stakeholders actually absorb the strategy — presenting it directly, tailoring the message to the specific audience in the room, and creating space for real discussion is usually the only reliable way to get genuine understanding and buy-in, rather than just circulated agreement.

A strategy document that buries its most important recommendation on page twelve is optimized for the writer's thoroughness, not the reader's attention — and a busy executive's attention is the actual scarce resource being spent.

Exercise

The scenario: An IA team has prepared a strategy presentation for senior leadership at a financial services firm. The presentation opens with fifteen minutes of detailed research methodology before mentioning any recommendation. The single most important recommendation — restructuring the entire product navigation — doesn't appear until slide 40 of 45. Throughout, research findings are presented as raw data tables with statistical terminology, and no slide explicitly connects any recommendation back to a business outcome like revenue, retention, or cost.

1. What problem does opening with fifteen minutes of research methodology before any recommendation create?
2. What problem does presenting findings as raw data tables with statistical terminology create for this audience?

Feedback

1 — It buries the most important recommendation too late. An executive audience's attention is limited and valuable; a strategy document or presentation is meant to open with the key issues and recommendations up front specifically so busy stakeholders get the essential message even if they can't stay for or absorb every detail. Waiting until slide 40 of 45 to reveal the single most consequential recommendation all but guarantees that some of the room has mentally checked out, or the meeting has run out of time, before it ever arrives.

2 — It frames the research inaccessibly for this audience. Raw data tables and statistical terminology are the wrong register for senior business leadership, who need the implications of the research translated into terms they can act on — not a methodology lecture. This doesn't mean the research was flawed; it means the presentation failed to translate it for the room it was actually delivered to.

The structural fix is to reorganize the presentation so it opens with the overview — the key recommendation and its business impact — before any supporting detail, consistent with how the strategy document itself should be structured. Financial services leadership specifically would need to see each recommendation explicitly tied to a business outcome they already track: how the navigation restructuring is projected to affect conversion, retention, or support costs, not just described as a UX improvement in the abstract.

None of this means the underlying research or the recommendation itself is wrong — the presentation's structure and framing are the problem, and both are fixable without changing a single finding, simply by reordering what comes first and translating the findings into the language this specific audience already uses to make decisions.

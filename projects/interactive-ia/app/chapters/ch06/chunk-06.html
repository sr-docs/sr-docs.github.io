Competitive Content Analysis

A competitive content analysis follows five steps: identify your key competitors, prioritizing a handful of direct competitors over a longer indirect list; conduct a content inventory of each competitor's site; conduct a content audit rating the quality of their content; analyze the topics that content covers; and analyze trends across all the competitors together to spot the themes that keep recurring among the strongest content.

The goal isn't imitation: it's finding the gaps and differentiation opportunities that only become visible once you can see your own content next to what competitors are actually offering. This works best as a periodic exercise, ideally quarterly and at minimum annually, done by a small team of two or three analysts who each evaluate independently before comparing notes together.

A competitive content analysis isn't about copying what competitors do well: it's about finding the specific gap where you could do something they haven't.

Exercise

The scenario: A fintech startup is building a help and support center for a new personal finance product. They have three direct competitors whose support centers are publicly accessible. The startup's head of product wants to know what topics to cover, what content formats to prioritize, and where there's an opportunity to differentiate.

Feedback

Analyzing the topics that content covers across all three competitors is the correct next step. The five-step process runs in sequence: identify competitors, inventory their content, audit its quality, analyze topics, then analyze trends across everyone together. Jumping straight to writing content would skip the topic and trend analysis entirely, losing the whole point of doing a competitive analysis in the first place, understanding the landscape before adding to it.

The password-reset versus data-deletion pattern is a textbook trend-analysis finding. All three competitors converging on the same well-covered topic, password reset, shows a saturated, expected area where differentiation is unlikely to matter much to customers, since everyone already covers it adequately. The shared gap around what happens to a user's data on account closure is far more interesting: it's a topic that matters to a privacy-conscious financial product's users, and none of the competitors have claimed it.

The startup should treat this gap as a genuine differentiation opportunity, creating clear, thorough content on data handling after account closure, a topic competitors have collectively left unaddressed, rather than spending limited content-development effort competing on a topic like password reset where competitors already meet the bar.

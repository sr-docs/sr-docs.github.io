Content Mapping

Content mapping turns an inventory and audit into something visual — a representation of how pages and content objects relate to each other, which is often far easier to reason about than rows in a spreadsheet. A map might show an entire information hierarchy, just its upper levels, an individual section in detail, or a specific page type's internal components for content reuse. Teams often build these with boxes and arrows, or physically with Post-it notes on a wall that can be rearranged and photographed as the structure evolves.

A content map becomes most useful when paired with usage-data analytics — overlaying what's actually popular or struggling onto the visual structure reveals patterns that neither the map nor the analytics would show on their own.

A spreadsheet tells you what exists. A map tells you what's actually connected to what — and it's often the second question that reveals where a structure has quietly gone wrong.

Exercise

The scenario: A content map for a professional training company's website shows six sections. Five of the sections link richly to each other — course pages link to related courses, instructor bios, and testimonials. The sixth section, a resource library of downloadable guides, has no incoming or outgoing links to or from any other section; it exists as an isolated cluster on the map, reachable only through one link on the homepage. User research shows people rarely find the resource library, and once they do, they never navigate from it back into the course catalog.

What does the content map reveal here that the user complaints alone might not have made as clear?

Feedback

The resource library is structurally isolated, not just poorly labeled. This is exactly the kind of finding a content map surfaces that a spreadsheet or a list of user complaints might leave ambiguous. Complaints alone could plausibly be explained by unclear labeling, low content quality, or a mismatched audience — the map rules those alternative explanations out visually by showing that the section simply has no meaningful connections to the rest of the site, reachable only through a single homepage link.

This is precisely why content mapping earns its place alongside the inventory and audit: a spreadsheet could tell you the resource library exists and even that its pages are individually well written, but it wouldn't as immediately reveal that the section is cut off from the site's connective tissue. Seeing the isolated cluster on the map makes the structural nature of the problem obvious in a way rows of data alone often don't.

A reasonable fix is adding contextual links from relevant course pages to related guides in the resource library — for example, linking a specific course to the downloadable guide that complements it — and adding a link back from each guide to the course catalog or to a related course, so users who land in the resource library have a natural path back into the site rather than a dead end.

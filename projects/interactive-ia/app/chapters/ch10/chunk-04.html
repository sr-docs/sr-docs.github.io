Associative Links

Associative links live within a page's actual content, words, phrases, or images that connect a concept being discussed to related information elsewhere, potentially crossing structural boundaries entirely. Unlike structural navigation, they convey nothing about the overall hierarchy; they simply say "this is relevant to what you're reading right now." Links buried inline within a paragraph are easy for readers to miss entirely, which is why more noticeable patterns tend to work better: links on article titles or product names functioning as subheadings, or generic phrases like "Read more" or "See also" paired with a more specific link nearby, such as the article's actual title.

Grouped associative links, a "Related Articles" or "You Might Also Like" block set off visually from the main content, with a clear label of its own, make these connections even more visible, and are a common, effective way to encourage further exploration or additional purchases without cluttering the main reading experience.

A link that says "Click here" tells a reader nothing about what's on the other side of it, which defeats the entire purpose of putting a link there in the first place.

Exercise

The scenario: A technology news website uses several associative linking patterns. User behavior data shows some are driving significant engagement while others are almost completely ignored.

1. A grouped "Related Coverage" block appears at the end of every article, clearly labeled, listing three other article titles as links. This pattern drives strong click-through.
2. Deep within long paragraphs, individual words like "chip" or "regulation" are quietly turned into links to other articles, with no visual distinction beyond the underline. This pattern is almost never clicked.
3. Every article ends with the exact phrase "Click here" linking to the homepage. This pattern is almost never clicked.
Chunk complete
Feedback

1: Effective. A visually distinct, clearly labeled group of specific article-title links is exactly the pattern the source material identifies as more noticeable and effective, it doesn't ask readers to spot something buried in running text.

2: Ineffective. Inline links on single words within a long paragraph are precisely what the source material warns readers tend to miss, especially with no visual treatment beyond a plain underline to draw the eye.

3: Ineffective. "Click here" is a direct example of the kind of meaningless link label the source material specifically calls out, it conveys nothing about the destination, giving readers no reason to click and, for anyone using a screen reader that jumps between links, no information at all.

The "Related Coverage" pattern succeeded on two specific traits worth generalizing: it was visually grouped and set off from the surrounding content rather than hidden inline, and its individual links used specific, meaningful text, actual article titles, rather than a vague generic phrase. Applying both traits elsewhere would mean converting the buried mid-paragraph links into either a similar grouped block or, at minimum, giving them stronger visual treatment so they don't disappear into the body text, and replacing "Click here" with a label that actually describes the homepage or whatever specific destination is intended.
